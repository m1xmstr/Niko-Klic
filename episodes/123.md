# Episode 123 — Which Word Comes Next? LLM Tokens

**Status:** Scheduled October 20, 2026 at 10:00 AM Central; public playback and manual-English verification pending.
**Track:** LLMs and language. **Prerequisites:** [AI: rules and learned patterns](121.md), [ML: checking predictions](122.md).

## Learning goal
Explain that a text-generating large language model builds text by predicting tokens from the context it has. Tokens are pieces of text: sometimes whole words, sometimes parts of words or punctuation. A continuation can sound natural without being true.

## Story
In a storybook railway station, Nico pulls a signal lever and Klic rolls a word carriage into a sentence train. The words begin **The train is**. They first attach **red**, then try **blue**. Both make ordinary sentences. But their toy engine is visibly red, so the blue sentence does not match the scene.

The carriages illustrate text choices. **They are not a running LLM, measured model predictions, or real train control.** The alternatives are selected for teaching. An actual tokenizer was executed separately to check the token pieces below.

## Observed tokenization

The cached Qwen2.5-Coder tokenizer at revision `3dd939c621c08e5753d5b89f35a2642cd83b98ca` produced these exact pieces, with special tokens disabled. Quotation marks make leading spaces visible; they are not part of the tokens.

| Input | Decoded token pieces | Token IDs |
|---|---|---|
| `playground` | `"play"`, `"ground"` | 1363, 1951 |
| `The train is` | `"The"`, `" train"`, `" is"` | 785, 5426, 374 |
| ` red` | `" red"` | 2518 |
| ` blue` | `" blue"` | 6303 |
| `The train is red.` | `"The"`, `" train"`, `" is"`, `" red"`, `"."` | 785, 5426, 374, 2518, 13 |
| `The train is blue.` | `"The"`, `" train"`, `" is"`, `" blue"`, `"."` | 785, 5426, 374, 6303, 13 |

Encoding and decoding each example reproduced the exact input. Different tokenizers, or different surrounding text, can split words differently. “Play” plus “ground” is the observed split here, not a universal rule. A tokenizer splits text; it does not choose the next token. A language model supplies scores used to choose a continuation.

## Predict, reveal, check

- **What might follow “The train is”?** Both red and blue are possible sentence endings in our activity.
- **Does a token always mean a whole word?** No. Our observed `playground` example has two word-part tokens.
- **What changes when we swap red for blue?** The sentence's color claim changes; the engine itself stays red.
- **Why is the blue sentence wrong about this scene?** It disagrees with the visible red engine, even though it sounds natural.
- **Did these carriages run an LLM?** No. They are a teaching model of text choices. Only the tokenizer output shown above was executed.

## Grown-up-supported activity

Use paper and a red toy or drawing. Write **The**, **train**, **is**, **red**, and **blue** on separate cards. Arrange the first three, pause for a prediction, and try both endings. Read each sentence and compare it with the toy. Then use a blue drawing and check which sentence fits that changed scene. The first changed case changes the words; this optional second case changes the evidence.

Explain that using one card per word is a simple activity, not the rule for every real tokenizer. Split a separate **playground** card into **play** and **ground** to match our observed example. No account, personal information, paid tool, or software installation is needed.

For an optional technical reproduction, a grown-up can use the pinned tokenizer with Hugging Face Transformers: encode the exact inputs with `add_special_tokens=False`, decode each individual ID, and compare with the table. Preserve the leading spaces. No language-model generation is required to reproduce this tokenization check.

Real generation has many candidate tokens; this lesson's two alternatives are not a complete vocabulary or a probability measurement.
